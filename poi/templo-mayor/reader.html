Templo Mayor

Templo Mayor was the principal temple of Tenochtitlan, the Mexica capital. Two stairways climbed one pyramid to shrines for Tláloc, the rain god, and Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica patron god associated with war and the sun. The excavation beside the Metropolitan Cathedral exposes several generations of the pyramid and buildings from the surrounding sacred precinct.

Templo Mayor and the making of Tenochtitlan

When the Mexica enlarged their main temple, they built around the one that already stood there. An early sanctuary could disappear behind a new staircase and remain there for centuries. That building practice is why the excavation can tell the history of a city whose largest pyramid was dismantled after the conquest.

A capital on the lake

Mexica histories place the founding of Tenochtitlan around 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco. In the founding story, Huitzilopochtli directs the migrants to the place where they will settle. As the island settlement grew, causeways connected it to the mainland and canals carried people and goods through the city. The sacred precinct took shape near the center of this network.

At the top of its main pyramid, the north shrine honored Tláloc, whose rains could bring a harvest or ruin one. The south shrine honored Huitzilopochtli, the city's patron. Each had its own stairway. The House of the Eagles, smaller shrines and other buildings belonged to the same precinct and served distinct ceremonies.

The first shrines we can see

Historical accounts associate the first temple with the founding of Tenochtitlan. The oldest excavated phase that visitors can identify is called Stage II. INAH places it between the late 1300s and the early 1400s. Parts of both shrines survive from this phase. A reclining figure holding a vessel, called a chacmool, stood at the entrance to Tláloc’s shrine. A rectangular sacrificial stone stood at the entrance on Huitzilopochtli’s side. Later builders retained this north–south arrangement even as the pyramid grew.

How did builders enlarge the temple?

They covered an existing building partly or entirely with fill and new masonry, then laid a new outer face and stairs. Some campaigns enlarged only one part of the structure. Archaeologists group the work into seven major stages and give substantial additions lettered labels. IVb is a later addition to Stage IV.

How can archaeologists date a wall?

They first work out the order of construction. A stairway sealed behind a later wall was already there when that wall went up. A sealed deposit can tie pottery or other datable material to a building event. A date glyph or a historical account can help narrow the date. The masonry establishes the sequence. Assigning a stage to a ruler requires additional evidence.

How the empire furnished its temple

In the late 1420s, Tenochtitlan joined Texcoco and Tlacopan in the Triple Alliance. After the defeat of Azcapotzalco, the alliance expanded and drew tribute from conquered provinces. The tribute roll in the Codex Mendoza lists what places owed. Tochpan, near the Gulf Coast, owed garments, feathered shields and warrior costumes, among other goods. The roll records obligations. The deposits buried at the temple show what priests actually set aside for the gods.

The temple grew with the alliance. An enlargement under Motecuhzoma I bears the date glyph 1 Rabbit, corresponding to 1454. A later ruler, Axayácatl (1469–1481), is associated with the extension of the front platform that archaeologists call Stage IVb.

At the foot of the south stair of that platform lay a stone disk carved with the dismembered Coyolxauhqui. In the Nahua account of Huitzilopochtli’s birth, he kills his sister Coyolxauhqui on the mountain of Coatepec and sends her body down the slope. The disk’s position gave the story a place in the building. The stair rose toward his shrine and her body lay at its base.

Under Ahuízotl, the temple was rededicated in 1487, a date recorded on a preconquest dedication stone. Archaeologists associate a deposit known as Offering 125 with this period. Beneath its stone lid they found a dressed canid and flint knives at the bottom, a thick layer of marine animals above them, and more objects including two golden eagles near the top. That order records a sequence of actions by the people who buried the offering. The marine species also show that some materials came from far beyond the lake basin. Trade and tribute are both possible routes for individual items.

Why are the stone figures on the older stair disputed?

Excavators found figures on the older Stage III south stair in a deposit made when that phase was closed. The findspot helped suggest that they were Coyolxauhqui’s brothers. Diego Matadamas and Michelle De Anda Roge studied surviving pigment and found painted attributes associated with rain and pulque deities as well. The painting supplies evidence about the figures that their position on the stair alone cannot resolve.

Inside the House of the Eagles

While rulers enlarged the main pyramid, builders also reworked a separate complex just to its north. The House of the Eagles had roofed rooms, stone benches and a columned portico. The benches carry reliefs of figures in procession. Excavators found life-size clay figures dressed as eagles inside. INAH interprets the rooms as spaces for more restricted rites, including offerings and penance. Their benches and portico also recall buildings at Tula, the older Toltec capital. In this building the Mexica drew on an architectural tradition that predated their own empire.

The massacre at Tóxcatl and the fall of the city

The temple had reached its final phase when Hernán Cortés entered Tenochtitlan in 1519. In May 1520, while Cortés was away, Pedro de Alvarado’s men attacked people celebrating Tóxcatl in the sacred precinct. In the Nahuatl account recorded in Book 12 of the Florentine Codex, participants were singing and dancing when the Spaniards blocked the exits. The account was written down decades later by Nahua authors working with Bernardino de Sahagún. It preserves details about the ceremony and the assault from the perspective of the people whose city was under attack.

Fighting drove the Spaniards and their allies out of Tenochtitlan that year. They returned with a coalition that included Tlaxcalans, Texcocans and people from other Indigenous communities. After a siege, Cuauhtémoc was captured and the city fell on August 13, 1521. The Spaniards dismantled the main temple. Over the following centuries, streets and buildings covered the precinct’s surviving foundations. The excavation and the neighboring cathedral now occupy adjoining ground in the old ceremonial center.

What Mexico City dug up

In 1913, archaeologist Manuel Gamio uncovered the pyramid’s southwest corner. In February 1978, electrical workers digging near República de Argentina and República de Guatemala struck the carved Coyolxauhqui disk. The disk’s findspot at the south stair gave archaeologists a fixed point from which to work out the surrounding architecture. Excavations led by Eduardo Matos Moctezuma from 1978 to 1982 exposed successive phases of the temple and adjoining buildings. The site museum opened in 1987.

Work continued under the streets. In 2006, archaeologists found a relief of the earth deity Tlaltecuhtli in a property at the Argentina and Guatemala corner. They traced the slab to the floor associated with an enlargement under Ahuízotl. The carved stone still carried traces of paint. Its discovery led to excavation around the slab, including the deposits that help explain how offerings were assembled and buried.

Plaza Manuel Gamio

Stand at the public side of Plaza Manuel Gamio, beside the east wall of the cathedral complex. From the Zócalo’s northeast corner, go north on Calle de Seminario with the cathedral on your left.

Look for the excavated stone walls ahead and to your right as you reach the plaza. The pyramid is the large, stepped mass in the middle of the excavation. The House of the Eagles is a separate building on its north side, toward Calle de Justo Sierra.

For the next stop, use the signed Templo Mayor entrance beside the plaza at Seminario 8.

The two sides of the temple

Stand at the visitor path in front of the pyramid’s west face. Face the broad steps in the area marked Etapa IVb.

Look for the round Coyolxauhqui replica low on your right, at the foot of the south stair. On your left, look for two stone frogs crouching on a low altar.

See a close-up of the frog altar

What it tells you Where a sculpture stood matters. Coyolxauhqui lay below the shrine of the brother who threw her down a mountain in the story of his birth. The frogs mark the rain god’s half of the same building.

The stairs inside the pyramid

Stand at the signed Stage III and Stage II viewpoints farther along the marked path. You are moving inward through older layers of the same pyramid.

Look for the row of human figures leaning back against the south steps of Stage III, just inside the later front. At the roofed Stage II shrines, look for the reclining figure holding a vessel in front of Tláloc’s shrine if it is visible from the path.

What it tells you The older stair’s steps run into later masonry. That physical relationship gives archaeologists an order of construction. Excavators found the figures in a deposit made as this phase closed.

The House of the Eagles

Stand at the permitted viewpoint marked Casa de las Águilas on the north side of the pyramid, toward Justo Sierra.

Look for low rooms under a protective roof. Follow a bench along the base of a wall and look for the red-painted reliefs of figures in procession where they are visible. You can recognize the building by its rooms and benches even from a restricted viewpoint.

What it tells you Compare these enclosed rooms with the broad front stairs of the pyramid. The layout helps explain why archaeologists associate the building with rites for a smaller group.

The original Coyolxauhqui stone

Stand at the Coyolxauhqui viewing area in the site museum. Follow the signs for Sala 4, Huitzilopochtli.

Look for the torso in the center of the disk, with the head and limbs arranged around it. On her cheek, look for the bell that helps identify her. At the outdoor stop the replica showed you where the relief lay. Here you can read the carving on the original.

If you have time Look for the Tlaltecuhtli relief near the museum entrance. Use the label to locate where that slab was found. Both stones come from the foot of the pyramid and from different phases of its construction.
